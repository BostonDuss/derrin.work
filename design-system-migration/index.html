ServiceNow, 2023–24  ·  Digital Product Manager, Digital Experience Platform

Design System Migration

Nobody migrates 4,000+ pages by migrating 4,000+ pages. My job was to make them automatable, then sequence them against a release calendar I was also running.

Company ServiceNow
Surface servicenow.com
Years 2023–24
Role Digital Product Manager, Digital Experience Platform. Contract, Nov 2023–Dec 2024

The situation

ServiceNow had a new design system and over 4,000 pages that predated it. Every one of them was off-brand and not optimized for user experience, analytics, or SEO, and impossible to update at any reasonable speed. The design system was the fix on the UX side: content modeling, page functionality, and mobile viewing and functionality. Our engineer's estimate for migrating them by hand was five hours a page (and that estimate was conservative), which is ten person-years of work nobody was ever going to fund.

My role

I joined at the start, and the first thing I did was reject the framing. The mandate was "migrate 4,000 pages," but 4,000 is a number that tells you what kind of problem you have: you can't do it by doing it. So the actual work was making the pages automatable, and then covering the gap where a script can't reach. I was also orchestrating the semi-annual platform releases at the same time, which meant I was the only person who could see both calendars. That's why the migration sequenced around the releases instead of into them.

What I did

Translated a design system into machine-readable rules. Partnered directly with engineering to source and define requirements precise enough to build an automation script against. That meant converting specifications written for human designers into logic a script could follow without judgment.

Kept humans on the parts humans are for. Coordinated Studio to create new assets from old ones where the spec couldn't simply be applied, and brought copy and design into UAT, because the only way to know whether a script got a page right is to have the people who care about pages look at it.

Made performance the default rule for sequencing, and knew which exceptions were real. Every stakeholder believed their pages were the highest priority. I sequenced batches and geo markets against user data instead of volume of asking. But a rule that can't flex isn't a rule: pages supporting an upcoming release or a content update already in flight got moved up. My team cares about this page didn't.

Absorbed script defects without stalling the program. We'd surface pages that broke the automation. That meant getting the fix into the script, re-cutting the batch order, and going back to stakeholders to explain why what I'd committed to last week had changed. Doing that repeatedly without spending credibility is most of the job.

What happened

4,000+ legacy pages migrated to SEO-optimized design system templates that reinforced ServiceNow's brand identity

20,000+ manual hours eliminated. That's our engineer's estimate: five hours a page, roughly ten person-years of work that never had to happen

A better experience for reading the content, on mobile especially: modeled content, working page functionality, and layouts built for the screens most of the traffic arrives on

Stronger SEO and a unified brand across the pages that had been dragging on both

Site architecture stopped being the constraint on everything downstream

The script outlived the project. It became the model for migrating other legacy page templates onto the design system, which we went on to do.

I'd rather spend two weeks finding problems than spend six months explaining them.

What I'd do differently

I let the batch order get ahead of what I knew about the pages. We discovered script-breaking edge cases in flight, and each one cost a re-cut and a round of re-explaining to stakeholders I'd just finished aligning. A broader sample audit up front, deliberately hunting for the weird pages before locking sequence, would have surfaced most of those defects when they were cheap and spared the churn. I'd rather spend two weeks finding problems than spend six months explaining them.
